National Disturbance Chronology

Every national slowdown since 1948 that did not reach the recession bar: dated by one fixed rule and graded on the same damage scale as recessions.

At a glance

The line is the four NBER coincident series (payroll employment, industrial production, real income less transfers, real manufacturing and trade sales) combined by the Conference Board method and measured against a smooth trend. A disturbance is a stretch in which the line falls (activity grows more slowly than its trend, or shrinks) that stopped short of a recession.

Grade: the program's damage grade (labor, output and production), by the same rule as every recession: on the fall of activity itself, or on the fall against trend where activity fell less than 1 percent. 1.00 or more is a recession. No labels; the number is the grade. Confirmed: the trough is at least 14 months old. Newer dates can still move as data are revised.

How a disturbance is decided

1. Dated

The Conference Board's coincident index (payroll employment, real income less transfers, industrial production, real manufacturing and trade sales) against its 75-month moving average (the last three years extended by a forecast). Peaks and troughs by the Bry-Boschan rule. The Conference Board's published dates (Zarnowitz and Ozyildirim) stand through 2000.

2. Real

The index falls at least 0.95 point against its trend and ends below it.

3. Not a recession

The window contains no NBER recession month.

4. Confirmed

Once its trough is 14 months old.

Today

Several episodes often called slowdowns leave no mark on the composite at all and so are not here: the 1987 stock-market crash, the 1998 financial crisis, the 2004–05 soft patch and the March 2023 bank failures. Financial stress that never reaches jobs, output, income and sales is not a disturbance by this rule.
